Evidence that changes decisions

A Samhita principle

Evidence should change decisions. If measurement only tells us what happened after a program has ended, we have used it too late.

Our methods

Program evaluation methods

  • We run baseline, midline, and endline evaluations on a sample of people, to build a full picture of what an intervention actually changed, not just what it delivered.

  • We match the sampling method to what each cohort allows, random or stratified, since most of our programs run without a formal control group to compare against.

  • We capture composite impact through quantitative measures, so the aggregate effect of an intervention is measurable.

  • We add in-depth interviews, case studies, and focus group discussions with a selected sample, to surface the nuanced processes a number alone can't show.

  • We build indices like the Income Index, Economic Empowerment Index, and Resilience Index, so empowerment and resilience get tracked with the same rigour as income.

Proof of the climb

In partnership with Flipkart

Most CSR reporting stops at outputs: how many people attended a training, how many centres got built. That's useful for tracking delivery. It says little about what actually changed in someone's life.

Flipkart's program is a useful example of how our framework closes that gap, because it runs five interventions at once, and every one of them is tied to a specific outcome in a person’s life.

  • Financial and digital literacy training wasn't tracked as "training completed." We tracked whether people opened a dedicated, goal-based savings account afterward, because that behaviour is what proves the training changed how someone actually manages money.
  • Skilling for employment, delivered through spoken tutorials in multiple languages, was tracked through job placement and income change after the course, not attendance.
  • Healthcare and menstrual hygiene interventions, including sanitary napkin kiosks and affordable diagnostics, were tracked against awareness and actual use of services, since access to a service and a change in health behaviour are two different things.
  • Entrepreneurship training was tracked against the specific skills and mindsets micro-entrepreneurs could demonstrate afterward.
  • Early childhood education, delivered through Anganwadi Bal Vidyalayas, was tracked against something most programs miss entirely: whether it freed up a mother's time to work.

That's what person-first measurement means in practice. It starts from whether the person receiving a service is measurably better off, and picks the indicator that actually proves it, rather than the one that's easiest to count.

It's also continuous. Each of these chains gets revisited at baseline, midline, and endline, on the same people, so we can see whether early gains hold, fade, or compound over the life of the program. A number that looks strong at midline and drops by endline tells a very different story than one that keeps climbing, and only continuous tracking catches that difference.

In partnership with Avendus and IndusInd Bank
4Dimensions measured

We realised something was missing while measuring the outcomes of our own credit and livelihood programs. Loan repayment told us a borrower could pay on time. It told us almost nothing about whether her life had actually changed, whether she'd gained more say over her own income, or whether she could survive the next bad season.

Existing empowerment tools didn't close that gap either. Most were built for one context and broke when applied to another. Some were rigorous enough for a research paper but too complex to run at program scale. Others were simple enough to run at scale but missed the real complexity of a person's life. Data collected across different geographies, occupations, and projects rarely lined up well enough to compare. And gendered realities, the different constraints women face around money, mobility, and decision-making, often got folded into generic questions that missed them entirely. Even when a score came back, it rarely told a program team what to change next.

So we built our own. The Empowerment and Resilience Index measures four dimensions, statistically validated and weighted differently depending on the cohort. For women, decision-making agency carries the heaviest weight, 38%: her control over income use, savings, household purchases, and group participation, the clearest signal of whether she's making the choices or someone else is making them for her. Economic productivity and dynamism (29%) tracks revenue and reinvestment, whether a business or farm is actually growing. Digital and financial inclusion (17%) measures access to a mobile phone, an independent bank account, and digital payments, the basic infrastructure for engaging with modern markets. Freedom of mobility (16%), tracked specifically for women, measures whether she can travel alone beyond her village, a strong signal of the social autonomy everything else depends on.

Each cohort, women or men, entrepreneurs or workers, agricultural or non-agricultural, gets a version of the index built around their specific constraints, while staying comparable across gender, occupation, and sector. That's what generic tools couldn't do: tailored enough to be accurate, standardised enough to compare.
